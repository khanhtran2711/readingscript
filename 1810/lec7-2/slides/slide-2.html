Hypothesis Testing

Approximately 9 minutes

It is a formal statistical procedure for making inferences about a population from sample data.

The process begins with two competing statements:

Null hypothesis — H₀

and

Alternative hypothesis — Hₐ.

our final statistical decision is normally either:

reject H₀

or

fail to reject H₀.

We normally do not say that we have “accepted” H₀.

Let's make this much simpler.

Imagine I tell you:

“Studying while listening to music improves exam scores.”

That is a claim.

How could we investigate it?

We could collect data from students who study with music and students who study in silence.

But before analysing anything, we need to clearly define what we are testing.

Null hypothesis

The null hypothesis normally represents:

no effect,

no difference,

or

no association.

So in our example:

H₀: There is no difference in exam performance between students who study with music and students who study in silence.

Alternative hypothesis

The alternative represents the effect, difference, or relationship that we are investigating.

So:

Hₐ: There is a difference in exam performance between the two groups.

Why do we need H₀?

Students sometimes ask:

“Why don't we just test whether my idea is correct?”

Because statistics works differently.

We begin with a reference assumption.

The null hypothesis gives us that reference.

Think of H₀ as saying:

“For the moment, assume nothing interesting happened.”

Then we ask:

“Is our observed evidence sufficiently inconsistent with that assumption?”

If yes, we may reject H₀.

Don't worry about memorising all of them immediately.

Think of the process as a story.

First:

What are we claiming?

Then:

What would we expect if H₀ were true?

Then:

How unusual is our observed sample under that assumption?

Then:

Is it unusual enough to reject H₀?

Web analytics example

Suppose normal weekly conversion rate is:

5%.

You redesign the checkout page.

After deployment, your sample shows:

5.4%.

Question:

Did the redesign really improve conversions?

Or could 5.4% occur simply because the particular users visiting this week happened to behave differently?

That is exactly the kind of question hypothesis testing is designed to investigate.

Student question

Suppose I observe:

Old conversion rate = 5%.

New conversion rate = 5.01%.

Would you immediately claim that the new design is better?

Probably not.

Now suppose:

Old = 5%.

New = 25%.

Would that look interesting?

Absolutely.

But even then, as analysts, we should investigate:

quy mô mẫu, độ biến thiên, chất lượng dữ liệu, và bằng chứng thống kê.

That is the professional mindset I want you to develop.
