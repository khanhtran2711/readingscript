Slide 13

p-Value, α and Statistical Decision

Approximately 10 minutes

This is the most important technical slide in this lecture.

The materials introduce:

the significance level,

test statistic,

p-value,

and comparison between p and α.

Let's go slowly.

Step 1 – Choose α

Alpha, written:

[ \alpha ]

is the significance threshold.

Common values in the notes include:

0.05,

0.01,

0.10.

Suppose:

[ \alpha=0.05 ]

This gives us a decision threshold.

Step 2 – Calculate a test statistic

Depending on the analysis, we may perform:

a t-test,

Z-test,

Chi-square test,

or another appropriate statistical test.

The statistical procedure generates a test statistic.

Step 3 – Obtain the p-value

The supporting notes define the p-value as the probability of obtaining the observed sample result, or something more extreme, given that H₀ is true.

That final condition is essential.

Correct interpretation

Suppose:

[ p=0.035 ]

You can say:

“Assuming the null hypothesis is true, the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the observed one is approximately 3.5%.”

That is the correct statistical interpretation.

Lecturer Clarification – What a p-value is NOT

The PowerPoint contains wording such as:

“there is a 3.5% chance your results could be random.”

Do not teach students that interpretation literally.

A p-value is not:

the probability that H₀ is true,

the probability that H₀ is false,

the probability that your result happened “by chance,”

or the probability that your research hypothesis is correct.

The p-value is calculated under the assumption that H₀ is true.

So always condition the explanation:

“If H₀ were true…”

This phrase will protect you from many p-value mistakes.

Step 4 – Compare p with α

Decision rule:

If:

[ p\leq\alpha ]

we:

Reject H₀.

If:

[ p>\alpha ]

we:

Fail to reject H₀.

Example from the notes

Suppose:

[ \alpha=0.05 ]

and:

[ p=0.02 ]

Because:

[ 0.02<0.05 ]

we reject H₀.

The result is statistically significant at the 5% level.

Easy memory trick

Students often reverse the decision.

Remember:

Small p → evidence against H₀.

If p is tiny, the observed result looks difficult to explain under H₀.

Example 1

[ p=0.003 ]

[ \alpha=0.05 ]

Decision?

0.003 < 0.05.

Reject H₀.

Example 2

[ p=0.40 ]

[ \alpha=0.05 ]

Decision?

0.40 > 0.05.

Fail to reject H₀.

Do we say:

“H₀ is proven”?

No.

We say:

The sample does not provide sufficient evidence to reject H₀.

Example 3 – Very close

[ p=0.051 ]

[ \alpha=0.05 ]

Technically:

0.051 > 0.05.

So under this rule:

fail to reject H₀.

Now:

[ p=0.049 ]

Technically:

reject H₀.

But notice how close these values are.

Would you say:

0.049 = amazing strong evidence,

but

0.051 = absolutely no evidence?

No.

This demonstrates why analysts should not treat 0.05 as a magical cliff.

The actual p-value and broader evidence should still be reported.

Type I Error

The notes connect α = 0.05 to the risk of rejecting H₀ when H₀ is actually true.

That is called a:

Type I error.

Think:

False alarm.

You conclude an effect exists.

But actually it does not.

Example

H₀:

The new website design has no effect.

You reject H₀ and announce:

“The redesign works!”

But in reality, the redesign does not affect the population.

That is a Type I error.
