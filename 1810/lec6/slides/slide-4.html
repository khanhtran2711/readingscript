Graphs and the SALT Principle

Approximately 7 minutes

According to the lecture materials, every graph should contain the basic SALT components:

S – Scale A – Axes L – Labels T – Title

I want you to remember this because it is an easy checklist.

Before submitting a graph, ask yourself:

“Is my graph SALTy?”

Not salty because you're angry about your assignment.

SALTy because it has Scale, Axes, Labels and Title.

S – Scale

The scale determines how values are represented.

For example, suppose sales are:

98, 100, 101 and 103.

If I make the y-axis begin at 97, the differences may look enormous.

Someone looking quickly might think sales have exploded.

But the real difference is small.

So scale affects perception.

please note that scale should be consistent and appropriate and should not exaggerate or minimise the data it can mislead the audience.

For bar charts, a zero baseline is normally important because bar length itself represents magnitude.

For some line charts or scatterplots, starting at zero is not always necessary, but the scale must consistent.

A – Axes

The material describes the horizontal x-axis and vertical y-axis and associates them with independent and dependent variables.

For example:

Hours studied → x-axis.

Exam score → y-axis.

Why?

Because we might investigate whether changes in study time are associated with changes in exam performance.

L – Labels

Never make your audience guess what an axis means.

The notes specifically emphasise including appropriate units.

So instead of:

Sales

prefer something like:

Monthly Sales (£000)

if values are in thousands of pounds.

T – Title

The title should tell readers what they are looking at.

“Graph 1” is not informative.

“Sales” is slightly better.

But:

Monthly Online Sales, January–December 2026

is much more useful.
