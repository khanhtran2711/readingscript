Slide 3

Tables

Approximately 5 minutes

The slide reminds us that a table should identify its title, column headings, values and appropriate decimal precision, while remaining simple and clear. The accompanying notes add guidance about titles, headings, alignment, rules, footnotes and layout.

Students sometimes think:

“A table is just rows and columns. What could possibly go wrong?”

Quite a lot.

Consider this table:

ProductRevenue
Laptop25341.237849
Mouse435.4
Keyboard8732.5894

Technically, the values might be correct.

But visually it is inconsistent.

Why do we need six decimal places for one product and one decimal place for another?

If this is revenue, perhaps two decimal places are sufficient.

So good presentation is not only about correctness.

It is also about readability.

Another question: When should I use a table instead of a graph?

Tables are particularly useful when your reader needs precise values.

Imagine someone asks:

“What was revenue in March?”

If you need the exact figure, the table may be more useful.

But if someone asks:

“Is revenue generally increasing?”

a line chart will probably communicate the trend faster.

That gives us a useful distinction:

Table → exact values.

Graph → patterns, trends, comparisons and relationships.

Neither is automatically better.

The choice depends on the task.

Transition: Once we decide to use a graph, we need a simple quality check to ensure the graph is understandable. The slides give us a memorable rule: SALT.
