Slide 2

Presentation Using Graphics and Tables

Approximately 8 minutes

The fundamental message on this slide is that graphs and tables help us understand the information contained in data, but the appropriate graph depends on the type of data being represented.

There is one question I want you to develop the habit of asking before creating any visualisation:

“What type of data am I working with, and what question am I trying to answer?”

Do not begin with:

“I like pie charts.”

Do not begin with:

“This chart looks beautiful.”

And definitely do not begin with:

“I saw this graph on TikTok yesterday.”

Start with the data and the analytical question.

The accompanying notes divide our choices into several situations: categorical data, discrete or continuous numerical data, time-series data, and relationships between variables.

Let's make this practical.

Suppose I have a university dataset.

One variable is:

Major: Computing, Business, Engineering, Design

What kind of variable is that?

Allow students to respond.

Categorical.

If I want to compare how many students belong to each major, what would be sensible?

A bar chart.

Now imagine another variable:

Student height in centimetres.

Height is continuous numerical data.

If I want to investigate the distribution of student height, a histogram becomes much more useful.

Now another variable:

Monthly website visitors from January to December.

What is important here?

Time.

Therefore, a line chart becomes a natural choice because we want to observe change over time.

And finally:

Hours studied and exam score.

Here I have two numerical variables and want to investigate whether they are related.

A scatterplot is appropriate.

So notice something.

The graph is not chosen randomly.

The question determines what relationship, comparison, distribution, or trend we need to communicate.

Quick classification activity

Display or say each variable and ask students to answer verbally.

Favourite social-media platform.

Categorical.

Number of messages received today.

Discrete numerical.

Time spent on TikTok today.

Continuous numerical, depending on measurement precision.

Daily website visitors over the last 30 days.

Time-series data.

Hours spent studying and final exam score.

Two numerical variables—we might investigate them using a scatterplot.

This classification step might seem simple, but in professional analytics projects it prevents many bad visualisations.

For example, imagine a business analyst has customer age data.

If they create one bar for age 18, another for 19, another for 20, another for 21, all the way to 80, the chart could become very difficult to interpret.

Instead, if their question is:

“What does our customer age distribution look like?”

a histogram may communicate the answer much better.

Professional application

When building dashboards in tools such as Power BI, Tableau, Excel, R, or Python, the software may give you dozens of graph types.

The software can draw almost anything.

But the software does not automatically understand the analytical message you want to communicate.

That is your responsibility as the analyst.

A useful mental framework is:

Comparison → Bar chart

Distribution → Histogram or box plot

Relationship → Scatterplot

Trend over time → Line chart

Part of a whole → Pie chart, used carefully

We will develop each of these during today's lecture.

Transition: Before moving to graphs, however, we should not underestimate the simplest visualisation tool of all—the table.
