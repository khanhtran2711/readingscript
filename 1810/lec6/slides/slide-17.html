Slide 17

Line Chart

Approximately 4 minutes

The slide describes a line chart as a series of data points connected by line segments and notes that it is commonly used when observations vary through time—seconds, minutes, days, weeks, years or quarters.

The supporting document similarly identifies line charts as particularly suitable for time-series data and trends over time.

Imagine website traffic:

January: 10,000 visits.

February: 11,500.

March: 13,000.

April: 12,500.

May: 15,000.

A table gives us the exact figures.

A line chart gives us the story.

We can immediately see:

Overall growth.

Temporary decline.

Acceleration.

Seasonality, if enough periods are included.

Web analytics application

This is extremely common in your field.

You may track:

Daily users.

Weekly conversions.

Monthly revenue.

Bounce rate over time.

Page-load speed.

Advertising cost.

Email sign-ups.

Line charts help answer:

“How is this metric changing?”

Important consideration

Time normally goes on the horizontal axis.

And maintain consistent intervals.

If January and February are one centimetre apart, but February and March are five centimetres apart for no reason, the visualisation can mislead.

Student question

If I ask:

“Which social-media platform generated the most users this month?”

Line chart?

Probably not.

Bar chart might be better.

But if I ask:

“How has Instagram traffic changed during the last 12 months?”

Now a line chart makes much more sense.

Once again, notice the principle from the beginning of the lecture:

The analytical question determines the graph.

Transition to conclusion: And that takes us back to the question we started with: having data is not enough. We need to select a representation that helps people understand what the data is telling them.
