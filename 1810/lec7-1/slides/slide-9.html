Slide 9

Kurtosis

Approximately 7 minutes

The lecture materials introduce three categories:

Leptokurtic

Mesokurtic

Platykurtic

Let's understand them visually rather than memorising the words first.

Mesokurtic

This is used as the reference shape corresponding to the normal distribution.

Think:

ordinary bell shape.

Leptokurtic

The notes describe this as having:

a sharper peak,

heavier tails,

and more extreme observations.

The example has many values near 50 but also extreme values such as 100 and 2.

Platykurtic

This is described as flatter, with thinner tails and fewer extreme values compared with the reference normal distribution.

The observations are relatively more spread through the centre instead of being tightly concentrated with heavy tails.

Why do analysts care about the tails?

Suppose you work for a bank.

Most daily losses are small.

But occasionally the bank experiences an enormous loss.

Those rare extreme events matter enormously.

A distribution with heavier tails means extreme events can occur more often than a normal model would suggest.

Lecturer Clarification 2 – “Which distribution is better?”

No kurtosis category is inherently “better.”

We normally do not choose for our observed data to become leptokurtic, mesokurtic or platykurtic.

Instead, we observe the distribution we actually have and determine whether our analytical methods are appropriate.

That is the mindset I want you to develop as analysts.

Transition

We now understand the shape of a distribution. But how do we describe where one particular observation sits inside that distribution? That is where Z-scores become extremely useful.
