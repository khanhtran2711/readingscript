Slide 5

What are clickstream data

Teaching cue: 20 to 30. Reserve 2 minutes 30 seconds for the classification and bounce-rate exercises. Write an event, a parameter, a metric, and a KPI as separate examples. Explain the slide’s familiar terms, then update the GA4 meaning of bounce rate.

A useful working definition is that clickstream data is a time-ordered record of observed interactions during a digital journey. The original definition emphasises entering a page and eventually leaving. In practice, our records may cover only part of that journey, and modern event collection includes actions within a page as well as movement between pages.

The slide lists page views, purchases, bounce rate, time spent on a page, and popular pages. These belong at different levels of analysis. Separating those levels will prevent confusion later.

An event is an individual occurrence. For example, “A page was viewed at 10:15” or “An order was confirmed at 10:19.” A parameter describes the occurrence. For a product event, a parameter might identify the product. For a purchase, parameters might include the transaction identifier and currency.

A metric is a numerical measurement, often calculated by counting or summarising records. The number of page views is a metric. Revenue over a period is a metric. A list of the most popular pages is a ranking based on a selected metric. We must choose what “popular” means: most views, most users, or perhaps the most completed purchases associated with the page.

A key performance indicator, or KPI, is a metric selected because it helps assess an objective. Imagine UniCart wants more visitors who begin checkout to finish their purchase. We could use checkout completion rate as a KPI. We would need to agree exactly how to calculate it.

Suppose 100 observed sessions begin checkout, and 40 of those same sessions contain a confirmed purchase later in the sequence. Our teaching definition gives a checkout completion rate of 40 percent. If we instead divide the number of orders by all sessions on the website, we are answering a different question. Neither denominator should remain hidden in the report.

This also explains why counting clicks can mislead us. One visitor might press a button three times. Those are potentially three recorded actions by one observed visitor. If the business wants to know how many visitors attempted checkout, counting every click would answer the wrong question.

Take these examples: “Product H24 was added at 10:15”, “H24”, “240 basket additions this week”, and “Increase the share of checkout sessions with a confirmed purchase.” Which is an event, which is a parameter value, which is a metric, and which describes a performance objective that needs a KPI?

Teaching cue: Give one minute. Answers follow the order in the question. For the last item, ask students to name checkout completion rate as the possible KPI and to specify its denominator.

GA4 uses the term key event for an event identified as particularly important to the business. For example, a confirmed purchase can represent an important outcome for UniCart. [24]

Now we need to update bounce rate. In GA4, bounce rate is the percentage of sessions that are not engaged. With the default engagement time threshold, a session qualifies as engaged if it lasts longer than 10 seconds, contains a key event, or includes at least two page or screen views. Meeting any one condition is enough. The time threshold can be configured, so check the property’s settings. [2, 21]

A student could spend 30 seconds reading one page and leave. Under those assumptions, that session can be engaged even with only one page view. Therefore, “left after one page” is not an adequate GA4 bounce definition. If 80 of 100 sessions qualify as engaged, the engagement rate is 80 percent and the bounce rate is 20 percent. [2]

Teaching cue: Give students 90 seconds. Ask whether each session is engaged under the default threshold: one page for 30 seconds, no key event; one page for five seconds, no key event; two page views within five seconds. Answers: yes, no, yes. Have students name the qualifying condition.

What about time spent on a page? Elapsed clock time and active attention are different. You may open a tab, go for lunch, and return later. GA4’s engagement timing reflects time when a webpage has focus or an app is in the foreground. That still does not prove that the user understood or enjoyed the content. [3]

For UniCart, a long time on the delivery page might mean careful reading. It might also mean confusing information. To judge the experience, we would combine timing with the task, subsequent actions, and possibly a usability study.

When presenting any metric, explain what it counts, the period it covers, and the population in the denominator. That explanation makes a result reproducible and helps other people judge whether it answers the business question.
