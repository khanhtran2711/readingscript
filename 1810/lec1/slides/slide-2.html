Slide 2

Clickstream data collection techniques

Teaching cue: 03 to 08. Use the sea-of-data image as an illustration of volume. Give pairs one minute to list actions they could take in an online shop. Take two answers, then continue.

The slide describes the internet as a sea of data. That image helps us imagine the amount of information involved. For today, we need to look more closely at what kind of information we mean.

A product page contains information that a business publishes: the product name, price, description, and available sizes or colours. When a visitor uses that page, another kind of data can arise. They might open the size guide, watch a demonstration, select a colour, or press “Add to basket.” These actions help us study how the website is being used.

Take one minute with the person beside you. Imagine buying headphones on UniCart. List five things you might do before paying. Include at least one action that does not take you to a new page.

Teaching cue: After the minute, invite examples. Suitable answers include searching, filtering, viewing a product, playing a video, opening delivery information, and adding to a basket.

Notice how much can happen without changing pages. A person can compare colours, open a delivery panel, and attempt a purchase while the browser still displays the same address. That is why a list of page addresses may leave out important parts of the experience.

In analytics, we can represent an action as an event. An event is a record that something happened at a particular time. For example, the website could record that a visitor added product H24 to the basket at 10:15. Details attached to that event might describe the product, quantity, and page.

However, the fact that an action happened does not guarantee that we have a record of it. Someone must configure the collection correctly. A request might fail, a browser might restrict collection, or the website might never have been instrumented for that particular action. Instrumentation means adding and configuring the mechanisms that produce the records we need.

The scope also matters. Our own website’s analytics does not reveal everything a person did elsewhere on the internet. We may receive some information about how they arrived, but we do not automatically receive their full activity in a social app, a search engine, or another shop.

For our project, the useful question is: which observable actions would help us understand UniCart’s purchasing problem? That question will keep us focused as the available data becomes larger.
