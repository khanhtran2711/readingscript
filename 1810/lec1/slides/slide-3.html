Slide 3

Digital footprints and business intelligence

Teaching cue: 08 to 14. Allow 90 seconds for pairs to discuss the two students below. On the board, write “Observation”, “Possible explanation”, and “Next evidence”.

The slide calls these records digital footprints. A footprint tells us that something passed through a place. It does not explain every reason for the journey. Clickstream data works in a similar way: it provides evidence of recorded behaviour, while intentions require interpretation.

Imagine two students looking at the same expensive headphones. One is saving up to buy them. The other is checking a present for a friend. They both open the product page, inspect the pictures, and leave. Their recorded actions could look very similar even though their reasons are different.

Discuss this with your partner: what could we reasonably say from those records, and what would we need to ask or observe before making a stronger claim?

Teaching cue: Accept that both viewed the product and neither has a recorded purchase in the observed journey. Challenge answers such as “They could not afford it” or “They disliked the product” by asking what evidence supports the claim.

We can say the records show product-page views. We can also say we have not observed a purchase within the period we examined. A claim about affordability, satisfaction, or personal preferences would need more evidence.

Business intelligence means using information to support business decisions. In our UniCart case, the decision might be whether to investigate the checkout, improve delivery information, or change a campaign. A useful analysis connects a finding to a decision that someone can actually make.

Suppose the records show that many observed sessions reach checkout, but a smaller number contain a completed purchase. We now have a place to investigate. Possible explanations include a technical error, an unexpected fee, or visitors who are still comparing options. Another possibility is that the purchase event itself is missing. The analyst should examine the collection before blaming the customer experience.

We could split the data by device category to see whether the pattern differs between mobile and desktop. That is segmentation: examining a meaningful subset. We might find an association between mobile sessions and lower completion. That finding guides a test on mobile devices. It does not, by itself, prove that a particular button caused the difference.

In a professional project, I would record the business question, the evidence currently available, and the next action that could reduce uncertainty. If we suspect hidden delivery charges, we might inspect when the charge appears and observe users completing the task. If we suspect a payment fault, we would compare the analytics events with the relevant system records.

This is also a useful habit when using an AI assistant to summarise data. Ask it to separate observations from possible explanations. Then check both against the underlying records. A fluent explanation can still rest on an unsupported assumption.
