Practical session with Google Analytics

Teaching cue: 78 to 87. Use one minute to introduce the task, four minutes for pairs to work, two minutes for the debrief, and two minutes to set the follow-up work. The table below is a fictional prepared dataset, so the activity works without accounts or live data.

UniCart’s manager returns with an initial report. The table describes 100 observed sessions in one seven-day period. Every session in this cohort viewed a product. Each later row counts sessions that reached that step after the preceding steps, in order, within the same session. Each session counts once per step.

We will assume the counts have been checked for this exercise. Your task is to interpret the pattern and then explain what you would verify in a real implementation.

Work with your partner. Calculate the percentage of checkout sessions that completed a purchase and the percentage of the original 100 sessions that completed the full sequence. Then identify the step with the largest proportional loss. Finally, propose one additional event and one collection test that would help investigate that loss. You have four minutes.

Teaching cue: Put the questions on the board. Ask pairs to write a finding and a next action in complete sentences. If a pair finishes early, ask which transition loses the largest absolute number of sessions and whether that is the same transition.

Let’s compare the calculations. Twelve of the 30 checkout sessions reached purchase, so checkout completion is 40 percent. Twelve of the original 100 sessions completed the sequence, giving a full-sequence completion rate of 12 percent.

The proportional losses are 40 percent from product view to basket addition, 50 percent from basket addition to checkout, and 60 percent from checkout to purchase. The largest proportional loss is therefore at the last transition. The first transition loses more sessions in absolute terms: 40 compared with 18 at the last transition. We need to specify what we mean by “largest.”

That pattern makes checkout a reasonable place to investigate. It does not prove that the design is bad. A defined checkout_error event could help identify failed attempts. We should also test whether purchase fires after successful confirmation and compare the event with the order record.

Teaching cue: Accept another well-justified event, such as a custom delivery_cost_shown event, if the pair defines its trigger and connects it to a hypothesis. Do not treat an event name as useful without explaining what observation it represents.

For the two-week follow-up, use week one to complete the current training on setup and collection, write a five-event measurement plan, and test at least one event on an authorised test site. If you do not have a site yet, complete the plan first and use the instructor’s teaching environment for the collection test.

In week two, use a GA4 property available to you or the official demo account to answer one business question. Record the property and date range, the metric definition, your finding, and one limitation. Complete the assigned current certification pathway and report the actual completion status.

The Google demo account is useful for analysing existing data. Collection testing belongs on a site you are authorised to instrument. Keep those activities separate in your practical evidence. [12]

Transition to the closing discussion: We began with a manager asking why visitors were not buying. You now have a way to turn that broad concern into records, checks, and a more defensible next action.

Whole lesson conclusion

Teaching cue: 87 to 90. Give students one minute to write the exit response, take one short answer, then deliver the conclusion. Stay on slide 14 or return to the title slide.

Before we finish, write two sentences: what evidence would you want before recommending a checkout redesign, and what collection error could make that evidence misleading?

Teaching cue: A suitable response asks for ordered checkout and confirmed-purchase events, plus error or usability evidence, and identifies a missing purchase trigger, a duplicated event, or an inappropriate denominator. Accept other well-reasoned answers.

Today, we followed UniCart’s problem from a business question to an event collection plan. We distinguished activity records from published page content, and individual events from the metrics calculated from them. We also saw why event definitions, identifiers, and session rules affect the story a report appears to tell.

The practical responsibility is to explain what the data represents and check how it was collected. A recorded action can support a finding. A claim about motivation or cause usually needs further evidence.

When you next open an analytics report, ask which decision it can support, which observations produced it, and what might be missing. Then use those answers to choose a proportionate next step. That is the approach you will carry into the practical and into your own analysis projects.
