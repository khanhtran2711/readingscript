Slide 7

Reading a click path

Teaching cue: 42 to 51. Allow three minutes for the record-reading task. Spend about one minute pointing to the labelled fields in the original screenshot. Explain its historical context without treating it as a current GA4 export.

This slide describes a click path as an ordered sequence of pages or links. Ordering matters. Looking at delivery information before adding a product to the basket suggests a different sequence from looking at it after a payment problem. Both sessions might visit the same pages, but their paths differ.

The screenshot labels a timestamp, an IP address, a URL, a registered-user identifier, and a location derived from an IP address. Treat it as an older illustration of fields that an event or log system might contain. It is not a reliable guide to the columns in a current GA4 export. The meaning of “SWID” here is specific to that source, rather than a standard GA4 field name.

A timestamp helps place records in order. In a real dataset, also check the time zone and whether the field means event time or arrival time. An event can arrive late. Two records may share a timestamp, so a sequence field or event identifier can sometimes be useful as well.

The URL tells us which resource or page is involved. A visitor identifier helps us associate records with an observed browser or an authenticated account, depending on the system. A session identifier groups an episode of activity according to a session definition. These fields serve different purposes.

GA4 web tagging commonly uses first-party cookies to help distinguish users and sessions. A browser identifier is not a guaranteed count of individual humans. One student using a phone and a laptop may appear through separate identifiers. Several people using a shared browser may also be difficult to distinguish. Clearing storage or changing consent can affect continuity. [10]

An authenticated User-ID is another mechanism. The site must supply an appropriate identifier when it knows the signed-in user. Google does not automatically discover that identity from every page load. Even with an account identifier, collection and identity rules need deliberate implementation. [20]

The IP address in the old screenshot needs a separate correction. Google states that Analytics does not log or store IP addresses. Other log systems can contain them, depending on configuration. IP-based location is an approximation, and an IP address is unsuitable as a guaranteed person identifier. A shared university network is an easy example of why. [8]

With your partner, answer three questions. What do we observe? What is one reasonable hypothesis? What further evidence should we check? You have two minutes, and then we will compare answers.

Teaching cue: Use one minute to debrief. Observation: the selected records show an item view, basket addition, checkout start, and a recorded payment timeout. Hypothesis: the timeout may have prevented completion. Further evidence: inspect test results, payment or order records, and later events. The absence of purchase in this excerpt does not establish that the customer never purchased.

We should not say, “The visitor definitely abandoned the order because they were unhappy.” We have evidence of a recorded timeout and an incomplete view of subsequent activity. Perhaps they retried successfully. Perhaps an order completed but our browser lost the response. The backend could help us resolve that uncertainty.

On a larger dataset, path exploration lets us inspect common sequences and branch points. In GA4, paths can use event names or page and screen information. Such a view helps us find a recurring pattern, but it still depends on the data and the exploration settings. [14]

Also be cautious about where the journey begins. A referrer or campaign parameter may indicate an arrival source. It does not provide a complete history of the user’s activity on that other platform. Missing referral information should remain a limitation in our explanation.
