Slide 11

Session duration engagement time and dwell time

Teaching cue: 46 to 51. Refer to the older screenshot’s average session duration of 00:01:15. Give students 45 seconds to discuss whether a longer registration time is desirable.

The screenshot highlights an average session duration of one minute and fifteen seconds. The slide then asks us to consider a registration form. That is a useful example because longer time can have more than one meaning.

If a student spends longer reading course information, they may be considering the options carefully. If the same student spends longer correcting unexplained errors in a short registration form, the extra time may reflect difficulty. We need to know the task before interpreting the duration.

Separate three terms. Average session duration summarises how long sessions last according to the measurement system. GA4 still exposes an average session duration metric, so it is inaccurate to say that this concept has disappeared entirely. [12]

Engagement time concerns measured time with the website in focus or the app in the foreground. For our website example, it is closer to active exposure than simply measuring the time between opening and closing a tab. Average engagement time and average engagement time per session use different denominators. Read the full metric label before comparing them. [11, 12]

Dwell time is commonly used in search discussion for the interval after a search-result click and before returning to the results. It is not a synonym for either of those analytics metrics. We should not relabel a GA4 duration value as an exact measurement of the visitor’s return to the search results. The measures describe different things.

Imagine UniCart’s average registration time rises from two minutes to three minutes. What additional evidence would help you decide whether that change is a problem?

Teaching cue: Suggested answers include completion rate, validation errors, successful-task time, abandonment stage, device differences, and direct observation of users. Ask whether people who never finished were included in the time calculation.

An average can also hide very different experiences. Most people might finish quickly while a smaller group struggles for a long time. Looking at a median or the distribution, together with completion and error information, can reveal that pattern more clearly.

For a form study, define the beginning and end of the task. You might measure from the first meaningful interaction to successful submission, then report incomplete attempts separately. Do not silently exclude everyone who failed and present the successful users’ time as the experience of the whole audience.

For UniCart, our decision is whether the form supports successful registration efficiently. A longer visit is useful evidence to investigate, but it is not an automatic target to maximise.
