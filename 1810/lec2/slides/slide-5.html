Reading the city and browser table

Teaching cue: 16 to 23. Use two minutes for individual calculations and one minute for pair comparison. Work through the weighted average on the board. Treat the displayed rates as exact for this classroom calculation.

First, calculate the total sessions for each city. Then identify the row with the most sessions and the row with the highest pages per session. Finally, decide whether this table tells us which city produces the most purchases.

Teaching cue: Allow the working time. San Francisco has 5,000 sessions and Berlin has 4,000. San Francisco with Chrome has the highest session count. Berlin with Chrome has the highest pages per session. Purchases are absent from the table.

Notice that “highest” depends on the column. The supplementary notes suggest that Chrome in Berlin leads both measures. It leads pages per session, but Chrome in San Francisco has more sessions. A professional report should name the metric rather than say one row “performs best”.

Now we have a slightly more interesting problem. What is the average pages per session for each city? For San Francisco, we should not simply add 3.5 and 4.1 and divide by two. The two rows contain different numbers of sessions.

Multiply each row’s sessions by its pages per session. San Francisco’s Chrome row represents 10,500 pageviews, and its Firefox row represents 8,200. Together, that is 18,700 pageviews across 5,000 sessions, giving 3.74 pages per session.

For Berlin, the pageview totals are 11,000, 2,500, and 4,700. That gives 18,200 pageviews across 4,000 sessions, or 4.55 pages per session. Across the whole table, we have 36,900 pageviews across 9,000 sessions, giving 4.10. This is a weighted average because each row contributes according to its session count.

Real reports may display rounded averages, so reconstructing their totals this way can produce small differences. Whenever possible, use the underlying counts.

Berlin’s sessions involve more pageviews on average in this table. That is the observation. Greater interest is one possible explanation. Confusing navigation is another. Perhaps people are comparing products, or perhaps they cannot find the delivery policy. We need outcomes and journey evidence before deciding which explanation fits.

For a professional follow-up, I would compare purchase outcomes and relevant errors by browser, while checking page mix and traffic source. I would also confirm the date range and collection consistency before attributing the difference to the browser itself.
