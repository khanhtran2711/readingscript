Slide 3

Metrics and the unit being counted

Teaching cue: 07 to 12. Explain the counts slowly. Allow one minute for the three-user example and its debrief. Introduce the supplementary business measures briefly, reserving detailed cost calculations for slides 16 and 17.

A metric is a numerical measurement. It can be a count, an amount of money, an average, or a rate. “Twenty purchases” is a count. “Six hundred thousand đồng of revenue” is an amount. “Two percent of sessions led to a purchase” is a rate.

The questions on the slide sound simple: how many people downloaded something, purchased, or registered? However, each question contains a choice. Are we counting actions, identified users, or visits? Those answers can differ even when they come from exactly the same activity.

Imagine Linh visits UniCart in the morning and again that evening. Minh visits once. An also visits once. We have three identified users and four sessions in our small example. Linh adds a product to the basket three times while comparing colours. Minh adds one product. An adds nothing. How many basket-addition events occurred, and how many users added something?

Teaching cue: Pause. The answers are four add_to_cart events and two users who triggered add_to_cart. Ask a student to explain why the numbers differ.

That distinction prevents a common reporting mistake. If our dashboard shows four basket additions, we cannot announce that four customers are ready to buy. Repeated actions may come from the same person, and a basket addition is still only one stage of the journey.

The slide treats visitors and customers as equivalent. In a business project, keep those terms separate. A visitor may browse without buying. A customer must meet an agreed definition, such as having a confirmed order in the order system. Analytics user counts also depend on identification and the selected user metric. GA4 distinguishes total users, active users, new users, and returning users. They are not interchangeable labels. [2]

A session groups activity into a visit under the platform’s rules. In GA4, the default timeout follows thirty minutes of inactivity, and that setting can change. One user can therefore produce several sessions. A website identifier is also an imperfect representation of a person across browsers and devices. [3, 4]

The supplementary notes add business measures such as customer acquisition cost, lifetime value, ROI, and NPS. These can be useful, but a clickstream alone does not provide everything needed to calculate them. Costs may come from finance or advertising records. NPS comes from a recommendation survey. Its score is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors among valid responses. It is not a count of website clicks. [22]

The notes also mention conversion rate optimisation, or CRO. CRO is the work of improving a defined conversion rate through investigation and testing. The conversion rate is the metric. The optimisation programme is the activity.

Transition to slide 4: Counts tell us how much happened. Dimensions help us discover where the differences are.
