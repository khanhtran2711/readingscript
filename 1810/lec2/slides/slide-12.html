Slide 12

Goals and conversion rates

Teaching cue: 51 to 58. Use the four-row table on the slide for a one-minute calculation exercise. Explain the historical screenshot next, then return to UniCart. Allow one minute for a student to explain the denominator problem.

The goal is the intended outcome. A conversion is a qualifying completion of a defined action. The rate expresses how often that outcome occurred relative to an appropriate set of opportunities.

The small table gives us four calculations. Ten out of twenty is 50 percent. Ten out of one hundred is 10 percent. Fifty out of fifty is 100 percent. Five out of twenty is 25 percent.

Treat these as invented arithmetic examples. The brand names do not make the figures verified conversion rates for Primark, Netflix, YouTube, or Vimeo. The video example also needs an action such as a qualifying video play, rather than the generic column label “Number that bought”.

Could we rank those websites by the percentages? We would be comparing different actions and different audiences. Watching a free video and paying for a subscription involve different requirements. A high percentage can reflect an easy-to-trigger goal, a narrowly selected audience, or a collection error. It does not establish that the business model is more successful.

For a session-based purchase rate, count sessions with at least one qualifying purchase and divide by eligible sessions. If purchase is the selected GA4 key event, the session key event rate answers that kind of question. Selecting all key events instead would answer a broader question. [7]

For a user-based rate, we instead count distinct users who completed the qualifying action and divide by the defined eligible user population. Name that population. A metric labelled “Users” in one report may not mean the same thing as a total-user count in another. [2, 12]

Here is a separate small example. We have one hundred sessions, twelve purchase events, and eight sessions containing those purchases. What proportion of sessions purchased? It is eight percent. Twelve divided by one hundred describes purchase events per session. Repeated purchases mean that calculation does not give the percentage of purchasing sessions.

Now examine the historical screenshot. Its 60,652 goal completions divided by 97,838 sessions gives approximately 61.99 percent. The displayed calculation uses sessions. We cannot infer that 60,652 distinct people converted without knowing the selected goals and their counting rules.

The supplementary notes also subtract users from sessions and call the difference returning users. That subtraction does not count returning people. One person could account for several extra sessions. Likewise, 127,904 pageviews divided by 97,838 sessions is about 1.31 pages per session. Dividing by 75,263 users instead gives about 1.70 pageviews per user. The denominator changes the question.

Return to UniCart. Twenty purchasing sessions out of 1,000 gives two percent. Twenty-four out of 1,600 gives one and a half percent. Purchases increased by twenty percent, while the purchase rate fell by half a percentage point, or twenty-five percent relative to its previous level.

We can report both facts. To decide what to do, we need to examine which sessions changed and whether costs and order value changed too.
