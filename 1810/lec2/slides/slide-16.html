Slide 16

Customer lifetime value and calculated measures

Teaching cue: 71 to 76. Use the Week 5 tooltip in the original chart. Work through the hypothetical value calculation, allowing 45 seconds for students to calculate the contribution estimate. Treat the assumptions as a simplified model, not a forecast for a real business.

Customer lifetime value asks how much value a customer relationship generates or is expected to generate over a defined horizon. Before using the number, specify whether it represents revenue, contribution, or profit, and whether it is observed or predicted. Those choices change its meaning. [23]

The chart on this slide is an older lifetime-value report covering the first thirteen weeks after acquisition. In the Week 5 tooltip, revenue per user is A$16.47 and pageviews per user is 21.69. The two lines use different units and axes. Their vertical separation cannot be interpreted as a difference between directly comparable quantities.

The thirteen-week chart also does not show the full future lifetime of each customer. It describes an observation window. Revenue may accumulate as a cohort returns, but two rising cumulative lines do not establish that extra pageviews caused additional spending. We would need a more suitable analysis to investigate that relationship.

For a simple UniCart model, suppose an average customer spends 600,000 đồng per order, orders three times per year, and remains a customer for two years. Multiplying those assumptions gives 3.6 million đồng of expected revenue over that relationship. This is a simplified revenue estimate. It assumes stable purchase behaviour and does not discount future amounts.

Now suppose the contribution margin after the specified product and fulfilment costs is twenty-five percent. What contribution does that imply before acquisition costs and fixed overhead? It is 900,000 đồng. If we then spend 300,000 đồng acquiring the customer, we have 600,000 đồng remaining under this simplified model before the other excluded costs.

This is why comparing acquisition cost with revenue alone can mislead. The money received from a customer is not all available to pay for acquiring that customer. In a real project, finance should help define the cost coverage, refunds, retention assumptions, and prediction horizon.

The supplementary notes say that Google Analytics does not calculate CLV directly. We should make that statement more precise. GA4 has User lifetime explorations with measures such as lifetime revenue. It also has predictive metrics for eligible properties. Predicted revenue uses a defined future window, rather than promising an unrestricted lifetime profit forecast. These features do not automatically include every cost needed for the business’s own CLV model. [16, 17]

Finally, distinguish calculated metrics from custom metrics. A calculated metric combines existing measures using a formula. A GA4 custom metric represents numerical information collected through an event parameter. The slide’s phrase “customised metrics” covers ideas that have more specific meanings in the product. [18]
